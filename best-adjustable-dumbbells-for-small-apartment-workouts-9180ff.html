Who This Is For and What Problem It Solves

You live in a 600-square-foot apartment. You want to lift weights but don't have room for a full rack of dumbbells. You also have neighbors who will complain if you drop iron on the floor at 7 AM. This review is for you.

I tested five of the most popular adjustable dumbbell systems on the market over the last month. I measured footprint, noise levels, and how quickly I could change weights. I also checked build quality and whether the locking mechanisms hold up under heavy use. Here’s what I found.

Quick Summary

  • Who this is for: Apartment dwellers and anyone with limited floor space who wants a full-body strength workout without a rack.
  • Top pick: Bowflex SelectTech 552 — best balance of range, durability, and ease of use. It's the safest bet for most people.

What to Look For When Buying Adjustable Dumbbells for an Apartment

Not all adjustable dumbbells are created equal. Here’s what actually matters when you live in a small space.

Footprint and Cradle Size

Measure your floor space before you buy. The base (cradle) that holds the dumbbells can be almost as wide as a small coffee table. The PowerBlock Elite EXP has one of the smallest footprints because it uses a single cradle for both weights. The Bowflex 552 requires a wider base to accommodate the dial mechanism. If you're storing them under a couch or in a closet, check the dimensions of the base, not just the dumbbells themselves.

Max Weight Range

Most budget sets top out at 50 lbs per dumbbell. That's plenty for beginners and most intermediate lifters doing high-rep work. But if you're doing heavy rows or deadlifts, you'll want something that goes to 80 or 90 lbs. The PowerBlock Pro EXP can be expanded to 90 lbs per hand by buying additional adder weights. The Bowflex 1090 goes up to 90 lbs right out of the box, but it's huge and heavy.

Noise Levels

This is the big one for apartment living. Dial-based systems like the Bowflex are nearly silent when changing weights. Plate-loaded systems like the NUOBELL 80 require you to screw and unscrew collars, which is fast but creates a metallic clinking sound. The heavy steel plates on the Yes4All budget set will clang against each other if you're not careful. If you have downstairs neighbors, prioritize a dial system or use a thick rubber mat.

Locking Mechanism Reliability

This is a safety issue. A dumbbell that slips while you're pressing overhead is a disaster. The Bowflex uses a spring-loaded dial that clicks into place. The PowerBlock uses a magnetic safety pin. In my testing, both are rock solid. The budget options from Yes4All use threaded collars that can loosen over time if you don't tighten them properly. Check the lock mechanism on any set before every workout.

Weight Adjustment Speed

If you do circuit training, you don't want to spend 30 seconds changing weights between sets. The Bowflex and PowerBlock let you change weights in under 5 seconds. The NUOBELL takes about 10 seconds to unscrew and swap plates. The Yes4All takes even longer. For straight sets, speed doesn't matter. For HIIT or supersets, it does.

Individual Product Reviews

1. Bowflex SelectTech 552

The most popular adjustable dumbbell on the market, and for good reason. The 552 uses a dial system that lets you select weights from 5 to 52.5 lbs in 2.5-lb increments. Turn the dial, lift the handle, and the unused plates stay in the cradle.

In my testing, the adjustment is genuinely smooth. It takes about 3 seconds to go from 10 lbs to 40 lbs. The locking mechanism clicks audibly, so you know it's secure. The build quality is solid — these are plastic and metal, but they feel robust. The handles are textured and comfortable even during sweaty workouts.

The downsides? They're long. At about 16 inches, they're slightly longer than a standard dumbbell. This makes some exercises like close-grip presses feel a bit awkward. Also, the 52.5-lb max weight is limiting for advanced lifters. And the cradle is wide — about 24 inches across — which eats up floor space.

Verdict: The safest, most user-friendly option for most apartment dwellers. Buy these if you want a reliable set that works for a wide range of exercises.

2. PowerBlock Elite EXP

The PowerBlock is a completely different design. Instead of plates on a handle, it uses a system of rectangular steel blocks with a pin that you insert to select the weight. The Elite EXP goes from 5 to 50 lbs per hand in 2.5-lb increments.

The biggest advantage here is the footprint. The single cradle is about half the size of the Bowflex. You can slide it under a bed. The weight selection is fast — pull the pin, insert it into the weight you want, and lift. It's also arguably the most durable design because there are no plastic dials to break.

The downsides? The shape is square and bulky. It doesn't feel like a traditional dumbbell, so exercises like overhead tricep extensions require a different grip. The metal handles are knurled, which is great for grip but can be harsh on your palms without gloves. Also, the 50-lb max is a bit low for serious lifters.

Verdict: The best space-saving option. If floor space is your absolute top priority, get the PowerBlock. The weird shape takes a few workouts to get used to.

3. NUOBELL 80

The NUOBELL is the premium option. It looks and feels like a traditional dumbbell — round steel plates, a knurled chrome handle, and a weighted collar. The max weight is 80 lbs per hand, which is significantly more than the Bowflex or PowerBlock.

Weight changes are done by unscrewing the end cap, sliding off plates, and replacing them. It takes about 10 seconds per dumbbell, which is slower than the dial systems. But the tradeoff is that these feel like real dumbbells. They're balanced, they don't roll around, and they handle heavy loads without flexing.

The downsides are the noise and the price. The plates clink against each other when you set them down, and the metal-on-metal contact is louder than the Bowflex. They're also the most expensive set in this review, typically retailing for over $600. And at 80 lbs max, they're heavier than most people need.

Verdict: The best choice for serious lifters who want a traditional feel. Just be prepared to spend more and make a little more noise.

4. Bowflex SelectTech 1090

The 1090 is the big brother to the 552. It goes from 10 to 90 lbs per hand in 5-lb increments. That's a huge range that eliminates the need for a separate set of heavy dumbbells for most people.

The adjustment mechanism is the same dial system as the 552, and it works just as well. The build quality is impressive — these are built to handle serious weight. The handles are thicker and more substantial, which is good for heavy lifting but might be uncomfortable for people with smaller hands.

The downsides are significant for apartment living. These are massive. At 90 lbs, each dumbbell is over 17 inches long and weighs as much as a small child. The cradle is also enormous — it takes up a serious chunk of floor space. And the 5-lb increments mean you can't fine-tune your weight for smaller exercises like lateral raises.

Verdict: Buy these only if you have a dedicated workout corner and you actually need to lift 90 lbs. For most people, the 552 is a better fit.

5. Yes4All Adjustable Dumbbells (Budget Pick)

This is a no-frills, budget-friendly option. It's basically a set of metal plates that you thread onto a handle with a collar. And that's it. No dials, no pins, no fancy mechanics.

The pros are the price — you can often find these for under $150 — and the weight range. You can add as many plates as you want up to 200 lbs per dumbbell if you buy the expansion packs. That's more than any other set in this review.

The cons are numerous. The adjustment process is slow and tedious. The plates clang and clatter loudly. The handles are thick and can be slippery when you sweat. And the locking collars can loosen during exercise if you don't tighten them enough. In my testing, I had to stop a set of curls to retighten the collar, which is annoying and potentially dangerous.

Verdict: Only buy these if you're on a strict budget and you don't mind the noise and hassle. Spend the extra money on a better system if you can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are adjustable dumbbells safe for apartment floors?

Yes, but you should always use a rubber mat. Standard dumbbells can dent hardwood or crack tiles if you drop them. A 3/8-inch thick rubber mat will protect your floor and reduce noise for your neighbors.

How much space do I need for a home gym with adjustable dumbbells?

You need about a 6x6-foot area to comfortably do most exercises. That includes room for a bench, a mat, and the dumbbell cradle. If you're just doing standing exercises, you can get away with a 4x4-foot space.

Can I do a full-body workout with just adjustable dumbbells?

Yes. You can do squats, deadlifts, lunges, presses, rows, curls, and flyes with a single pair of adjustable dumbbells. The only thing you can't do well is barbell-specific lifts like a heavy back squat or a bench press with a barbell.

What's the difference between the Bowflex 552 and 1090?

The 552 goes up to 52.5 lbs per hand and has 2.5-lb increments. The 1090 goes up to 90 lbs per hand but uses 5-lb increments. The 1090 is also significantly larger and heavier. For most apartment dwellers, the 552 is the better choice.

Bottom Line Verdict

If you want the best all-around adjustable dumbbell for a small apartment, get the Bowflex SelectTech 552. It's quiet, reliable, easy to use, and covers the weight range that 90% of people need. If floor space is your absolute top priority and you can handle the square shape, get the PowerBlock Elite EXP. If you're a serious lifter who needs up to 80 lbs and wants a traditional dumbbell feel, splurge on the NUOBELL 80. Skip the budget options unless you have no other choice — the noise and safety issues aren't worth saving a hundred bucks.
